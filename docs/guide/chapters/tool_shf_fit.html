What this is and when to reach for it

The SHF Fit pane turns the interpreted logs into a saturation-height function: a law that gives water saturation from height above the free-water level, so saturation can be carried away from well control. Reach for it when the study needs volumetrics between the wells, or a saturation model for a simulator that will never see a log.

Five families are offered: Cuddy's FOIL, Brooks-Corey, Skelt-Harrison, Thomeer's hyperbola, and Leverett's J-function. This chapter fits the Leverett J-function, for two reasons. It is the only one of the five that carries rock quality explicitly, through a permeability and porosity term, so one law can span rock of different quality instead of averaging across it. And its two coefficients are exactly what the forward sw_height module consumes, so the fit made here becomes the law that writes the curve.

The equation

J  = 0.21645 · (Pc / σcosθ) · √(k / φ)
Pc = 0.433 · (ρw − ρhc) · h        [psi, h in feet]
Sw = A · J^B

Read it from the bottom line up. Height above the free-water level becomes a capillary pressure through the density difference between the two fluids; that pressure becomes a dimensionless J through the interfacial tension and the rock's own √(k/φ); and saturation is a power law in J. The pane solves for A and B by least squares in log-log space. The J-function is Leverett (1941), Transactions AIME 142.

That √(k/φ) term is what earns the extra input curve. Two samples sitting the same height above the contact but in rock of different permeability land at different J, so the same law gives them different saturations. It is capillary physics doing the work rather than a curve shape.

The pane

  • Inputs are curves, not core: porosity, water saturation and a TVDSS curve, plus permeability for this family. The fit refuses, naming the reason, when the wells carry no usable TVDSS samples. A project with no deviation surveys has no height axis to fit against.
  • The fluid system fills in its own constants. Choosing Water-Gas sets σcosθ to 50 and the hydrocarbon density to 0.2 g/cc; Water-Oil sets 26 and 0.7. Both are the app's shipped values for those systems and both stay editable, because the fluids in your reservoir are yours to state.
  • Fit per rock type splits the fit by a rock-type curve and returns one A and B per class. On stacked sands that is the whole point of the exercise, not a refinement.
  • Every excluded sample is counted, by reason, under the plot; and the fitted curve on that plot is drawn at the median √(k/φ) of the samples in the fit, since one line has to stand for a range of rock quality.

The picks and why

  • Free-water level 1520.1 m TVDSS: the mean of the three gas-water contact picks in the fluid contacts registry, which fits them as one flat surface with no well flagged. The pane does not pick a contact for you, and should not: a contact comes from pressures, from the resistivity collapse, or from a registry of picks somebody made and can defend. Mind the depth reference: this field is TVDSS, and on these three wells measured depth runs exactly 30 m deeper throughout, so the same contact is 1550.1 m MD.
  • Min φ 0.10: the study's own porosity cutoff, the same number the pay summary applies, so shale never enters the fit.
  • Water-Gas: the contact is a gas-water contact, so the gas constants are the right pair.
  • RT_LOG as the rock-type curve, the three classes the cutoff rock types chapter writes.

Step by step

  1. Open the pane from the workspace ＋ menu (SHF Fit (Cuddy FOIL): the menu is named for the default family, and the form select changes it).
  2. Set SHF form to Leverett-J, point the four selects at the study's PHIE, SWE, TVDSS and PERM, and choose the fluid system.
  3. Enter the free-water level and the porosity cutoff, tick Fit per rock type with the rock-type curve, and press Fit SHF.
  4. Read the exclusion counters before the coefficients, then the per-class tabs before the pooled one.

Reading the result

Pooled over the three wells the law is Sw = 0.405 · J^−0.614, R² 0.639 with an rms of 0.270 over 503 points. B is negative, which is the first thing to check: saturation must fall as J rises, because J rises with height above the contact. Of the 1,164 samples the wells offered, 590 fell below the porosity cutoff, 65 sat at or below the free-water level, 4 carried a saturation of zero or less and 2 had no permeability. Each is counted rather than quietly dropped.

The per-class tabs are where the reading actually happens:

          A        B        rms      R2       points
pooled    0.40486  -0.61430  0.27022  0.63911  503
RT 1      0.42305  -0.69119  0.24219  0.68965  449
RT 2      0.89050  -0.00150  0.03422  0.00163   49
RT 3      0.90676  +0.18070  0.30909  0.14494    5

Three different verdicts, which one pooled R² hides. RT 1 is the law: 449 of the 503 points, a steeper B and a better fit than the pool, and it is the best rock in the ladder, so it deserves to dominate. RT 2 is a real statement, not a failed fit: B is effectively zero and saturation sits flat near 0.89 whatever the height, which is what capillary-bound rock looks like: it has no transition zone to model, and the rms of 0.034 says the flatness is tight rather than noisy. RT 3 is not a law at all: B comes back positive, which would mean saturation rising as you climb away from the water, and five points is simply not enough to ask. Quote RT 1, describe RT 2, and throw RT 3 away.

QC and pitfalls

  • Check the sign of B before anything else. A positive B is capillary physics running backwards; it means the class has too few points, or a rock-type curve that is mixing rock, not that the reservoir is unusual.
  • The free-water level is an input, not an output. The pane will scan for one, but a scan minimises a residual and a contact is a physical surface. Take it from pressures or from the contacts registry, and let the fit be a test of that pick rather than a substitute for it.
  • Fit above the free-water level only, and read the exclusions. A fit dominated by near-contact points measures the transition zone's noise instead of its shape, and the counters are how you notice.
  • One law per rock type when the entry pressures differ. A single J-function through two rock qualities averages two curves into neither, which is exactly what the pooled row above is doing to RT 2.
  • A J-function fitted from logs is not a J-function measured from core. This one inherits whatever the saturation curve already assumed; where SCAL capillary-pressure data exists, the Pc fit is the independent measurement to check it against.
